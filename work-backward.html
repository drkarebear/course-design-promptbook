Work Backward

Start with the destination you chose and identify the learning students will need before they are asked to do the culminating work independently.

The goal: turn the culminating task into a learning pathway so important skills are taught, modeled, practiced, and supported before they become high-stakes expectations.

Where you are

  1. 1 Find the Units
  2. 2 Design the Destinations
  3. 3 Work Backward
  4. 4 Map the Semester

Why this step matters

If it matters at the end, teach it before the end.

Culminating work often contains several invisible moves: decisions, habits, disciplinary ways of thinking, technical skills, or conventions that experienced faculty may perform automatically. Working backward makes those moves visible before students are graded on them.

  1. 🔎Deconstruct

    Identify the knowledge, skills, decisions, and moves hidden inside the destination.

  2. 🪜Sequence

    Move from instruction and modeling toward practice, feedback, and greater independence.

  3. 🔍Check

    Look for leaps, missing bridges, unnecessary steps, and skills students are expected to know but have not been taught.

Before you prompt

What you’ll need

  • Your selected destination brief from Step 2
  • The unit purpose and major learning
  • Your Course Outline of Record (COR)
  • Your course-specific alignment rubric, if you made one
  • Any relevant knowledge or skills students are expected to bring from an earlier unit or prerequisite

Keep this step pre-calendar. You are identifying the learning pathway, not deciding Week 5 versus Week 6 yet. The weekly schedule comes next.

Prompt 1

Unpack what the destination actually requires

Ask AI to make the invisible work visible before it starts inventing activities.

Deconstruct

Copy-and-paste prompt

I am working backward from this culminating task:

[Paste the destination brief from Step 2.]

Use the unit purpose, Course Outline of Record, and course-specific alignment rubric, if provided, as constraints.

Do not create activities or a weekly schedule yet.

First, identify the distinct things students must know or be able to do to complete this task successfully.

Group the requirements into course-appropriate categories such as:

  • knowledge or concepts students need to understand;
  • disciplinary thinking, analysis, interpretation, problem-solving, or decision-making;
  • technical, composing, research, performance, or production skills;
  • genre, format, process, or communication conventions;
  • and any important self-monitoring, revision, collaboration, or reflection moves that are genuinely part of the task.

Use only categories that fit this course and task. Do not force every category to appear.

For each requirement, briefly explain what students would need to be able to do, not just the name of the topic.

Distinguish between:

  • learning that should be taught in this unit;
  • learning that may reasonably carry forward from an earlier unit or prerequisite; and
  • anything that appears to be an unnecessary demand that does not meaningfully support the unit destination.

Keep the analysis concise enough to use as a planning tool.

👀

Faculty check

Look at the list before asking AI to scaffold it.

Remove anything that does not actually matter to the destination. Add any important disciplinary move AI missed. This is where your expertise keeps the pathway focused instead of letting the assignment quietly expand.

Prompt 2

Build the learning pathway

Now decide how students can move from first exposure toward independent performance.

Sequence the learning

Copy-and-paste prompt

Here is the refined list of learning students need before the culminating task:

[Paste the requirements you kept or revised from Prompt 1.]

Turn this into a concise learning pathway.

For each important skill or cluster of related skills, identify what students would likely need in a sensible progression:

  • Learn: the idea, process, strategy, convention, or skill that needs explicit instruction;
  • See: what would benefit from a model, demonstration, worked example, annotated example, think-aloud, or other visible example of expert practice;
  • Practice: what students should try in a lower-stakes or more focused form before the culminating task;
  • Get feedback: where useful feedback, self-checking, peer response, instructor response, or another feedback loop would help students improve;
  • Apply: what students should eventually do with increasing independence in the culminating work.

Do not mechanically require all five stages for every skill. Use only the support that the learning actually needs.

Where several skills can be taught or practiced together, combine them rather than creating unnecessary separate steps.

Do not create specific classroom activities, discussion prompts, assignment sheets, or a weekly schedule yet.

The result should show the instructional progression, not a calendar.

Prompt 3

Check for missing bridges and unnecessary steps

A good scaffold removes avoidable barriers without turning every small move into its own assignment.

Audit the pathway

Copy-and-paste prompt

Review this learning pathway for the culminating task:

[Paste the pathway from Prompt 2.]

Check it for four things:

  1. Missing bridges: places where students are expected to perform a meaningful skill before they have been taught, shown, or given a reasonable chance to practice it.
  2. Hidden expectations: disciplinary conventions, academic habits, technology use, directions, or other expectations an experienced professor might assume students already understand.
  3. Over-scaffolding: steps that repeat the same learning, over-direct students, or create extra work without meaningfully improving readiness.
  4. Misalignment: steps that do not clearly build toward the unit destination or an important COR expectation.

Recommend only the smallest useful changes.

Do not add work merely to make the pathway look more complete.

End with a short, revised pathway that preserves only the learning and support students actually need before the culminating task.

Quick quality check

Does the pathway actually prepare students?

✓ Good signs

  • The major demands of the culminating task appear somewhere in the learning pathway.
  • Important skills are taught before students are heavily graded on them.
  • Students get opportunities to see difficult work modeled when modeling would help.
  • Practice becomes more independent over time.
  • Feedback appears where students can still use it.
  • The pathway is focused enough that you can imagine fitting it into the unit.

! Watch for

  • The final task asks for a skill that never appears earlier.
  • AI mistakes assigning a task for teaching the skill.
  • Every skill gets five separate scaffolded activities whether it needs them or not.
  • The pathway quietly grows beyond the original unit purpose.
  • Students spend more time completing scaffolds than doing meaningful disciplinary work.
  • A step exists only because it sounds pedagogically impressive, not because it builds toward the destination.

The principle to carry forward

Practice before the big grade.

If students are graded heavily on a skill, you should be able to point to where they learned it, saw it demonstrated when useful, practiced it, and had a chance to improve before the culminating performance.

Not every skill needs a miniature assignment. Good backward design is not about creating more work. It is about making sure the work you do include prepares students for what comes next.

Repeat as needed

Do the same for each unit's destination.

Once one learning pathway looks right, repeat this process for the other units. You do not need every pathway fully polished yet. At the end of this step, you simply want a clear sense of what students need to learn, see, practice, and get feedback on before each culminating task.
