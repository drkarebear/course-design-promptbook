🛠 Step 6

Build the Learning

Create the activities, practice, and smaller assignments that help students actually learn the moves they will need later.

The goal: make students do the important thinking, give them useful practice before high-stakes work, and keep every learning experience connected to something that matters.

Where you are

  1. 4 Map the Semester
  2. 5 Match the OER
  3. 6 Build the Learning
  4. 7 Check the Build-Up

Why this step matters

A reading is not the same thing as learning.

Students need opportunities to notice, try, explain, make decisions, practice, get feedback, and try again. This step turns your backward map into actual learning experiences without filling the course with busywork.

  1. 💡Explore

    Generate several short activity possibilities before committing to one design.

  2. 🧭Choose + build

    Select the activity that best serves the learning need, then develop only that one.

  3. 🔍Adapt + check

    Fit it to the teaching context, accessibility needs, and student population without lowering the learning.

Before you prompt

Work from one real learning need.

  • The unit and week you are designing
  • The specific skill, concept, decision, or disciplinary move students need to practice
  • The culminating work or later task this learning supports
  • The OER or resource students will use, if one is relevant
  • Your teaching modality and realistic amount of class or student time

Start small: do not ask AI to generate an entire semester of activities at once. Design one meaningful learning need at a time so you can make better choices and keep the course coherent.

Prompt 1

Generate a few activity possibilities

Ask for different ways students could practice the same important learning.

Explore first

Copy-and-paste prompt

I am designing one learning experience for a college course. Do not build the full activity yet.

Course / unit:
[Paste the relevant course and unit context.]

Learning need:
[What students need to learn, practice, notice, decide, analyze, create, perform, or apply.]

This builds toward:
[Paste the culminating task, later assignment, or larger course ability this supports.]

Resource students may use:
[Paste the relevant OER section, example, data, text, media, or write “none.”]

Teaching context:
[In person, online asynchronous, online synchronous, hybrid, or other relevant context.]

Available time:
[Approximate minutes or student workload.]

Give me 4 meaningfully different activity ideas. Keep them brief enough to compare.

For each idea, include:

  • Activity name
  • What students actually do in 1–2 sentences
  • Why it supports this learning need
  • Approximate time
  • What evidence of learning I would see
  • How it builds toward the later work

Make the options genuinely different, not four versions of the same worksheet or discussion.

Prioritize active student thinking, purposeful practice, manageable cognitive load, and adult relevance.

Do not create all four activities in full. Stop after the short options so I can choose.

👉

Faculty decision point

Choose before AI builds.

Pick the idea that best fits your students, your teaching context, and what the activity needs to accomplish. You can also combine the strongest feature of one option with another. The point is to make the pedagogical choice before asking AI for the details.

Prompt 2

Develop only the activity you chose

Turn the selected idea into something you could realistically use or adapt.

Build the choice

Copy-and-paste prompt

I chose this activity idea:

[Paste the selected idea.]

Develop only this activity into a concise, ready-to-adapt teaching plan.

Include:

  1. Learning purpose — what students are practicing and why it matters.
  2. Student-facing directions — plain, transparent language that explains what to do.
  3. Activity sequence — the smallest number of steps needed for students to succeed.
  4. Materials or examples needed — only what is actually necessary.
  5. Instructor role — what I should model, clarify, observe, prompt, or respond to.
  6. Evidence of learning — what students will produce, say, decide, demonstrate, or revise.
  7. Feedback or debrief — a realistic way students learn from the attempt before later high-stakes work.
  8. Estimated time.
  9. Build-toward connection — one sentence explaining what later work this prepares students to do.

Keep the activity appropriately rigorous for a college course, but do not add complexity just to make it look rigorous.

Remove steps that do not meaningfully contribute to the learning purpose.

Use lenses only when they matter

Adapt the activity—not the whole course.

You do not need a separate version of every prompt for every teaching context. Once the activity itself is sound, apply the lens that actually changes how students will experience it.

🏫In person

Use live modeling, pair or group interaction, movement, whiteboards, or immediate instructor feedback when they serve the learning.

💻Online asynchronous

Make the task self-explanatory, navigable, realistically timed, and meaningfully connected to instructor or peer interaction when appropriate.

🎥Online synchronous / hybrid

Decide what truly benefits from live time and what students can do more effectively before or after the meeting.

♿Accessibility

Check directions, formats, interaction requirements, alternatives, reading order, media access, and whether students can demonstrate the learning without unnecessary barriers.

🎓Dual enrollment — when relevant

Preserve college-level outcomes while making hidden expectations, processes, timelines, and college conventions more visible. Do not make the work childish or easier.

Prompt 3

Adapt the activity for your teaching modality

Use this only after you have an activity worth adapting.

Fit the context

Copy-and-paste prompt

Adapt this activity for [in person / online asynchronous / online synchronous / hybrid]:

[Paste the activity.]

Preserve the same learning purpose and college-level rigor.

Revise only what needs to change for the modality.

Check:

  • whether the directions are clear without relying on unstated instructor explanation;
  • whether the interaction format genuinely helps the learning;
  • whether students have a clear way to begin, participate, and finish;
  • whether instructor presence or feedback is meaningful rather than decorative;
  • whether the technology or format adds unnecessary cognitive load;
  • whether the activity can be completed accessibly using keyboard, screen reader, captions/transcripts, and reasonable alternatives where relevant;
  • and whether the time estimate is realistic in this modality.

Do not merely digitize an in-person activity or add a discussion board by default.

Return the revised activity and briefly explain the 2–4 most important modality changes you made.

Optional prompt

Adapt an activity for dual enrollment

Use this only when the activity will actually be taught in a dual-enrollment section or mixed-age setting.

Optional lens

Copy-and-paste prompt

I will use this college-level activity with dual-enrollment students:

[Paste the activity.]

Adapt the activity only where the dual-enrollment context creates a meaningful need.

Do not lower the college-level learning outcomes, intellectual work, or expectations.

Look instead for places where students may benefit from:

  • more explicit directions or success criteria;
  • college vocabulary or conventions being briefly explained;
  • hidden curriculum being made visible;
  • a model, worked example, or low-stakes rehearsal before independent performance;
  • clearer time-management or multi-step planning support;
  • clarification of college-versus-high-school roles, communication, or deadlines when relevant;
  • and age-appropriate examples that still respect students as college learners.

Do not make the activity childish, oversimplified, or more heavily scaffolded than the learning actually requires.

Return the revised activity and a short note explaining what changed and why.

Quick quality check

Is the activity doing real instructional work?

✓ Good signs

  • Students—not the instructor—do most of the important thinking.
  • The activity practices something students will use again later.
  • The directions make the purpose and task clear.
  • The amount of scaffolding matches the difficulty of the learning.
  • Students produce some visible evidence of what they understand or can do.
  • There is a realistic feedback, reflection, or debrief opportunity when one is needed.
  • The activity fits the time and modality.

! Watch for

  • The activity is engaging but disconnected from later work.
  • Students spend more time following directions than thinking.
  • The instructor performs the difficult reasoning while students mostly watch.
  • Every reading automatically gets a worksheet, quiz, or discussion.
  • Technology makes a simple learning task harder to access.
  • A dual-enrollment adaptation lowers rigor instead of increasing transparency.
  • Extra steps or products appear because AI can generate them, not because students need them.

The principle to carry forward

Every activity should answer: What does this build toward?

You now have learning experiences connected to real course destinations. Step 7 checks the course as a sequence and looks for orphan assignments, abrupt jumps, duplicated work, and important skills that never receive enough practice.
