📚 Step 5

Match the OER

Choose readings and materials because they support the learning already on your map—not because they happen to be available in the book.

The goal: give each resource a clear instructional job, keep student workload realistic, and leave gaps visible instead of forcing a weak match.

Where you are

  1. 3 Work Backward
  2. 4 Map the Semester
  3. 5 Match the OER
  4. 6 Build the Learning

Why this step matters

The OER should serve the course—not become the course.

Once the weekly learning needs are clear, you can make much better choices about what students actually need to read, watch, examine, or keep as a reference. This is where a large resource becomes a purposeful course reading plan.

🎯Name the need

Identify what a resource needs to help students learn, notice, understand, or do.

🔎Match with purpose

Choose the specific section, chapter, video, example, or resource that best serves that need.

✂️Trim and flag gaps

Remove weak or redundant matches and keep unmet needs visible for later searching or instructor-created support.

Before you prompt

What you’ll need

  • Your weekly semester map from Step 4
  • The OER, textbook, open course, or other resource you are considering
  • A table of contents, chapter list, links, or the resource itself when possible
  • Any student reading-time or workload limits you want AI to respect
  • Any required materials or texts that the COR actually specifies

Important: if the resource does not teach something students need, do not force a match. Mark the gap clearly. A visible gap is more useful than a reading that only mentions the topic.

A useful way to think about resources

Give every assigned resource a job.

A reading or video does not need to do everything. What matters is that you know why students are using it.

TeachExplain a concept, process, strategy, or disciplinary move.
ModelShow what effective analysis, writing, performance, problem-solving, or practice looks like.
Provide contextGive background, cases, examples, perspectives, data, or primary material students need.
Support practiceProvide material students can work with as they apply a developing skill.
Serve as referenceGive students something useful to consult when they need it rather than assigning every page in advance.

Prompt 1

Turn the weekly map into resource needs

Before matching chapters, tell AI what each week actually needs from a resource.

Name the need

Copy-and-paste prompt

I have already mapped the learning for this course week by week. I am now deciding what readings and other resources students actually need.

Weekly course spine:
[Paste your semester map from Step 4.]

Do not choose resources yet.

For each week, identify the specific instructional need a resource could serve.

Use a concise table with:

  • Week / learning focus
  • What students need from a resource — for example explanation, model, context, example, practice material, primary text, data, or reference support
  • Why that resource would matter for the learning or build-toward in that week
  • Priority — Essential, Helpful, or Optional

Do not invent a resource need simply to fill every week. If a week does not need an assigned reading or outside resource, say so.

Keep the list lean. Focus on what students genuinely need in order to make progress toward the course destinations.

🧠

Faculty decision point

Decide what is essential before you start assigning pages.

If the resource list already looks long at the needs stage, trim now. Students do not benefit from being assigned everything that might be useful. Protect the materials that clearly help them learn or perform something important.

Prompt 2

Match the OER to those needs

Now ask AI to locate the strongest matches inside the resource you actually have.

Match with purpose

Copy-and-paste prompt

I want to match an existing OER or course resource to the resource needs I identified.

Resource-needs map:
[Paste the output from Prompt 1.]

OER or resource:
[Upload the resource, paste its table of contents or relevant sections, or provide the material you want analyzed.]

Student workload limit, if I have one:
[For example: approximately 45–60 minutes of assigned reading/viewing per week, or another realistic limit.]

Match the resource to the needs on my map.

For each proposed match, identify:

  • Week / learning need
  • Specific resource section — chapter, subsection, pages, video, example, or other identifiable location when the source supports that level of precision
  • Instructional role — Teach, Model, Context, Practice, or Reference
  • Why it fits in one concise sentence
  • Priority — Essential, Helpful, or Optional

Do not assign an entire chapter when a smaller section would meet the need.

Do not claim that a section teaches a skill merely because the topic appears there. Look for actual explanation, examples, modeling, guided support, or other useful instructional evidence when instruction is needed.

If the resource does not provide a strong match for a need, write No strong match rather than forcing one.

Do not invent page numbers, section titles, or resource content that you cannot verify.

When the OER has a gap

A missing match is information.

Do not rebuild the whole course around a weak resource. A gap may mean you need a different OER section, a library resource, a short instructor-created explanation, a model, or another form of support.

Keep the gap specific. “Need a better resource” is vague. “Need a concise model showing how students move from evidence to analysis before Week 6 practice” gives you something useful to search for or create.

Prompt 3

Trim the plan and turn gaps into search briefs

Finish this step with a resource plan that is purposeful, manageable, and honest about what is still missing.

Trim + flag gaps

Copy-and-paste prompt

Review this proposed OER/resource map:

[Paste the matches from Prompt 2.]

Help me make it leaner and more purposeful.

Check for:

  1. Weak matches: material that mentions a topic but does not actually provide the kind of support the week needs.
  2. Redundancy: multiple readings or resources doing essentially the same instructional job without a clear reason.
  3. Over-assignment: entire chapters or long resources when a smaller section would be sufficient.
  4. Low-value extras: material that is interesting but not meaningfully connected to the week's learning or build-toward.
  5. Unmet needs: places where students still lack an explanation, model, context, practice source, primary material, or reference they genuinely need.

Recommend removals before additions.

For every remaining unmet need, create a short resource search brief that states:

  • what students need to learn or do;
  • what kind of resource would help;
  • the level and scope needed;
  • and what would count as a strong match.

Do not invent a resource to fill the gap.

End with a revised week-by-week resource map containing only the strongest assigned materials plus the clearly labeled unresolved gaps.

Quick quality check

Does every assigned resource earn its place?

✓ Good signs

  • You can explain why each assigned resource is there.
  • Specific sections are assigned instead of whole chapters by default.
  • Important instructional needs have strong matches.
  • Optional or reference material is clearly distinguished from essential work.
  • Student reading/viewing load looks realistic for the week.
  • Unresolved gaps are visible and specific.

! Watch for

  • The OER table of contents has quietly become the course sequence.
  • Students are assigned a chapter because it exists, not because they need it.
  • A skill is assessed even though the resource only mentions it.
  • Every interesting supplementary item becomes required.
  • Reading load grows without anything else being removed.
  • AI fills a gap by inventing a source, page number, or section.

The principle to carry forward

Assign less. Make each resource matter more.

Now you know what students are learning each week and which materials support that learning. Step 6 can focus on the learning experiences students need to actually use those materials, practice important skills, and build toward the destinations.
