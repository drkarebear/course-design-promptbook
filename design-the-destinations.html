🎯 Step 2

Design the Destinations

Before you plan the weeks, decide what meaningful work students could produce, perform, analyze, or demonstrate at the end of each learning arc.

The goal: choose a clear destination for each unit so later readings, activities, practice, and feedback have something purposeful to build toward.

Where you are

  1. 0 Understand the Course
  2. 1 Find the Units
  3. 2 Design the Destinations

Why this step matters

Know where students are going before you build the road.

A culminating task gives a unit direction. It does not have to be a giant project or traditional paper. It should simply give students a meaningful way to show the learning that matters most in that part of the course.

✨Explore

Generate several brief, genuinely different destination ideas.

☝️Choose

Select the one that best fits the learning and the course you want to teach.

🎯Clarify

Define the destination clearly enough to work backward from it.

Before you prompt

What you’ll need

  • Your chosen 3–5 unit course map
  • Your Course Outline of Record (COR)
  • Your course-specific alignment rubric, if you made one
  • Any real constraints that affect what students can reasonably produce or perform

Brain-friendly tip: work on one unit at a time. Asking for 4 ideas for one unit is usually easier to evaluate than asking AI for 20 ideas across the whole course at once.

Prompt 1

Explore four possible destinations for one unit

Keep the ideas short at first. You are choosing a direction, not writing the assignment sheet yet.

Explore first

Copy-and-paste prompt

I am designing the culminating work for this unit:

[Paste the unit title, purpose, and major learning here.]

Use the attached Course Outline of Record and course-specific alignment rubric, if provided, as constraints.

Give me 4 meaningfully different culminating task ideas for this unit.

For each idea, briefly provide:

  • a clear name for the task;
  • what students would produce, perform, analyze, solve, create, or demonstrate;
  • the major learning the task would make visible; and
  • why the task fits this unit.

Keep each idea short enough to compare quickly.

Use forms of assessment that make sense for this particular discipline and COR. Do not force every idea to be a paper or presentation.

Do not build the assignment directions, rubric, weekly schedule, practice activities, or scaffolding yet.

Do not recommend one option as the best. Stop after presenting the four ideas so I can choose.

☝️

Faculty decision point

Pick the destination before you design the assignment.

Choose the idea that best captures the learning you want students to demonstrate. You can revise an option or combine elements, but resist the urge to ask AI to fully develop all four.

Prompt 2

Stress-test the destination you chose

Check the fit before investing time in directions, criteria, or scaffolding.

Check the choice

Copy-and-paste prompt

I want to use this culminating task for the unit:

[Paste the option you chose.]

Check it against the unit purpose, the COR, and the course-specific alignment rubric, if provided.

Tell me briefly:

  • what important unit learning this task would allow students to demonstrate;
  • whether any important unit learning would remain difficult to see or assess through this task;
  • whether the task asks students to do something substantially outside the intended learning of the unit; and
  • whether the scope appears realistic for a culminating task at this point in the course.

Do not redesign the task unless there is a meaningful problem. If a small adjustment would improve alignment or feasibility, suggest only that adjustment and explain why.

Do not create the rubric, scaffold, weekly plan, or assignment sheet yet.

Prompt 3

Turn the choice into a destination brief

Clarify the end point just enough that you can work backward from it in Step 3.

Clarify the destination

Copy-and-paste prompt

Develop the selected culminating task into a concise destination brief.

Include:

  • Purpose: why students are doing this work;
  • What students will produce or demonstrate: a clear description of the end product, performance, solution, analysis, or demonstration;
  • Learning made visible: the major knowledge, skills, or abilities students will need to demonstrate;
  • Connection to the unit and COR: the major course expectations this task supports; and
  • Scope: a concise description of what belongs in this task and what does not.

Keep the brief focused on the destination.

Do not create the detailed assignment directions, grading rubric, scaffolded practice, weekly sequence, or lesson activities yet. I will work backward from this destination next.

Quick quality check

Is it a useful destination?

✓ Good signs

  • The task makes important unit learning visible.
  • Students are doing something meaningful with what they learned.
  • The scope feels substantial but teachable.
  • You can already imagine what students will need to learn and practice first.
  • The task fits the discipline rather than following a generic AI assignment template.

! Watch for

  • The task is impressive-looking but only loosely connected to the unit learning.
  • The assignment requires major skills students have not been taught yet.
  • Every unit ends with the same kind of product for no clear reason.
  • The task has grown so large that the unit would become mostly assignment management.
  • AI has started writing detailed directions before you have decided what students actually need to demonstrate.

Repeat as needed

Do the same for the remaining units.

Once one destination feels right, repeat this process for the other learning arcs. You do not need the assignments fully written yet. At the end of this step, you simply want a clear destination for each major part of the course.

Culminating does not have to mean high-stakes. A destination can be a paper, project, performance, portfolio piece, case analysis, demonstration, solution, presentation, product, or another form of meaningful evidence appropriate to the course.
