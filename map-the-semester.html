🗓️ Step 4

Map the Semester

Turn your chosen units and learning pathways into a realistic weekly spine before you start filling the calendar with readings and activities.

The goal: give important learning enough time to develop while protecting students—and you—from a course that becomes overloaded simply because the calendar has boxes to fill.

Where you are

  1. 2 Design the Destinations
  2. 3 Work Backward
  3. 4 Map the Semester
  4. 5 Match the OER

Why this step matters

The calendar is a constraint, not the curriculum.

A weekly plan should reflect how learning develops—not force every topic into an equal-sized box. Some ideas need time to settle, some skills need repeated practice, and some culminating work needs room for feedback and revision.

📏Set the constraints

Use the real term length, meeting pattern, fixed dates, and unit destinations.

🧭Map the learning

Give each week a clear learning purpose and a visible connection to what students are building toward.

⚖️Stress-test the pace

Look for overload, rushed feedback, grading bottlenecks, and weeks trying to do too much.

Before you prompt

What you’ll need

  • Your selected 3–5 unit structure from Step 1
  • The destination or culminating work for each unit from Step 2
  • The learning pathways you developed in Step 3
  • Your actual term length, such as 16 weeks, 8 weeks, or another format
  • Known no-class days, holidays, final-exam requirements, or other fixed dates that affect instruction
  • Your modality or meeting pattern when it meaningfully affects pacing

Do not add the readings yet. First decide what students need to be learning and doing each week. Step 5 will help you match OER and other materials to those needs.

Prompt 1

Explore a few pacing possibilities

Keep the course structure fixed, but let AI show you a few sensible ways the available time could be distributed.

Explore pacing

Copy-and-paste prompt

I have already designed the major structure of this course. I am now deciding how to pace it across the term.

Course structure and unit destinations:
[Paste your units and destination briefs.]

Backward-designed learning pathways:
[Paste the learning pathways from Step 3.]

Term constraints:
[Enter the number of instructional weeks, meeting pattern or modality if relevant, known holidays/no-class dates, final-exam requirements, and any other fixed dates.]

Do not redesign the units or culminating work.

Do not assign readings, create activities, or write detailed assignments yet.

First, give me 2–3 brief, meaningfully different pacing options for distributing the existing units across the available instructional time.

For each option:

  • show approximately how much time each unit receives;
  • explain the pacing logic in 1–2 sentences;
  • identify where students would need meaningful practice, feedback, revision, or transition time;
  • and note the main tradeoff of that pacing choice.

Keep the options concise enough that I can compare them quickly.

Do not recommend a best option until I have had a chance to choose.

🧠

Faculty decision point

Choose the pacing logic before filling the weeks.

The question is not which option looks busiest or most complete. Choose the one that gives the most important learning enough time and still leaves room for students to practice, receive feedback, and recover when real life disrupts the calendar.

Prompt 2

Build the weekly spine

Now turn the chosen pacing option into a simple week-by-week learning map.

Build the spine

Copy-and-paste prompt

I want to use this pacing option:

[Paste the pacing option you selected or describe the parts you want to combine.]

Using the existing units, destination briefs, and backward-designed learning pathways, create a week-by-week course spine.

For each instructional week, identify:

  • Unit: which learning arc students are in;
  • Primary learning focus: the most important thing students are learning or getting better at that week;
  • Build-toward: what later destination, performance, or major skill this week prepares students for;
  • Learning stage: whether the emphasis is mainly learning, seeing a model, practicing, getting feedback, applying, revising, or transitioning;
  • Milestone: only when there is a meaningful checkpoint, draft, feedback window, culmination, or transition worth naming.

Keep each week focused. Prefer one primary learning emphasis, or at most two closely related emphases, rather than filling every week with a long list of topics.

Build in realistic time for feedback and revision before major culminating work when the course design calls for it.

Do not assign specific OER readings yet.

Do not create detailed activities or assignment instructions yet.

If a holiday, shortened week, or fixed date materially changes the plan, account for it rather than pretending it is a normal instructional week.

If your course is compressed

Do not just squeeze a 16-week course.

An 8-week or other compressed course still needs a coherent learning progression. Some learning experiences may need to be combined, narrowed, or made more efficient, but the sequence from instruction toward independent performance should remain visible.

Useful test: ask whether the compressed version preserves the important learning path—or simply asks students to complete the same amount of work twice as fast.

Prompt 3

Stress-test the semester map

Before adding readings and activities, check whether the weekly plan is actually teachable and learnable.

Check the pace

Copy-and-paste prompt

Review this weekly course spine:

[Paste the weekly map from Prompt 2.]

Stress-test it for realistic pacing and cognitive load.

Look specifically for:

  1. Overloaded weeks: too many new concepts, skills, major decisions, or high-stakes demands appearing at once.
  2. Rushed learning: important skills moving from first introduction to major assessment without enough time to practice or improve.
  3. Feedback bottlenecks: drafts, major assignments, or feedback-heavy work clustered in ways that are unrealistic for students or the instructor.
  4. Dead or disconnected weeks: weeks whose purpose is unclear or that do not meaningfully build toward later learning.
  5. Transition problems: abrupt jumps between units or culminating tasks with no time to consolidate, reflect, or reorient when that would help.
  6. Calendar fiction: holidays, shortened weeks, or final-exam periods treated as though they contain a full normal week of instruction.

Recommend only the smallest useful changes.

Do not solve pacing problems by adding more assignments.

Do not add readings or detailed activities yet.

End with a revised weekly spine that is as clear and lean as possible.

Quick quality check

Does the calendar serve the learning?

✓ Good signs

  • Each week has a clear learning purpose.
  • The sequence visibly builds toward the unit destinations.
  • Difficult skills get more than one encounter when they need it.
  • Feedback happens while students still have time to use it.
  • Major due dates are distributed realistically.
  • The plan has enough breathing room to survive an imperfect semester.

! Watch for

  • Every week contains five or six unrelated learning goals.
  • AI fills empty calendar space simply because the space exists.
  • New instruction continues right up to a major culmination with no practice or revision window.
  • Several feedback-heavy assignments land at the same time.
  • A holiday week carries the same workload as a full week.
  • The schedule looks organized but you cannot explain what each week builds toward.

The principle to carry forward

Give the learning a home before you give it a reading.

Your semester map now tells you what students need in each week. That makes the next step much easier: instead of assigning everything in an OER, you can look for the specific readings, examples, videos, or other resources that support the learning already on the map.
