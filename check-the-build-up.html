🔗 Step 7

Check the Build-Up

Trace the course in both directions: what does each task build toward, and where does each important culminating skill get taught and practiced first?

The goal: find orphan assignments, missing bridges, abrupt jumps in difficulty, redundant work, and feedback that arrives too late to help.

Where you are

Why this step matters

A good assignment can still be in the wrong course—or in the wrong place.

Once a course is full of readings, activities, quizzes, discussions, drafts, and projects, it becomes easy to lose sight of the sequence. This step checks whether the pieces actually prepare students for later learning.

  1. →Trace forward

    For each meaningful course element, ask: what does this build toward?

  2. ←Trace backward

    For each important culminating skill, ask: where did students learn and practice this first?

  3. ✂Repair lightly

    Remove, merge, move, or add only the smallest bridge the course actually needs.

A useful question

What does this build toward?

If an activity or assignment has no clear answer, it may be an orphan assignment: work that consumes student and faculty time without a clear role in the learning sequence.

Orphan does not automatically mean bad. A task may be interesting, engaging, or academically worthwhile and still not earn its place in this particular course. The question is whether it contributes enough to the course goals to justify the time it takes.

Prompt 1

Trace every important course element forward

Find out what each meaningful task is actually preparing students to do later.

Trace forward

Copy-and-paste prompt

I want to check whether the important activities and assignments in this course build toward later learning rather than functioning as disconnected tasks.

Course context / COR or alignment rubric:
[Paste or attach the relevant material.]

Course map, weekly plan, activities, and assignments:
[Paste the course sequence or attach the materials you want reviewed.]

Review the course in sequence.

For each meaningful activity, assignment, quiz, discussion, draft, practice task, or project, identify:

  • Course element
  • Where it occurs — unit/week/module if available
  • What students practice or demonstrate
  • What later learning or task it builds toward
  • Connection status — Connected / Weak connection / Possible orphan
  • Brief reason

Use a simple table.

Do not label routine logistical tasks, orientation items, or necessary low-stakes checks as orphans merely because they do not directly feed a major assignment. Use judgment about whether the task serves a legitimate course function.

Flag a Possible orphan only when the course element consumes meaningful student or faculty effort and its instructional purpose is unclear, redundant, or disconnected from later learning.

Do not redesign the course yet. Stop after the trace so I can review the pattern.

👀

Pause before fixing

Look at the pattern before accepting AI's repairs.

A “weak connection” is not automatically a problem. You may have a good reason for keeping an experience that serves community, confidence, exploration, transfer, or another legitimate course purpose. Decide what actually needs repair before asking AI to change anything.

Prompt 2

Trace the culminating skills backward

Now check the reverse direction: are students prepared before the grade gets big?

Trace backward

Copy-and-paste prompt

Now review the course from the culminating work backward.

Culminating assignments / major assessments:
[Paste the major tasks or use the course materials already provided.]

Identify the important skills, knowledge, decisions, and disciplinary moves students are expected to demonstrate in the culminating work.

For each one, show where students previously:

  • learn or encounter it;
  • see it modeled or made visible, when modeling is appropriate;
  • practice or apply it;
  • receive useful feedback, when feedback is needed before high-stakes use;
  • and use it in the culminating task.

Use a concise table with one row per important culminating skill or ability.

Then label each row:

  • Prepared — the sequence provides enough prior support;
  • Thin bridge — some support exists, but students may face a jump in difficulty;
  • Missing bridge — students are heavily graded on something they were not meaningfully prepared to do.

Do not mechanically require every skill to include modeling, multiple practices, and formal feedback. Use judgment based on the difficulty and importance of the learning.

Do not propose repairs yet. First show me where the sequence is strong and where the bridges are thin or missing.

Prompt 3

Make the smallest useful repairs

Trim first. Add only what the course truly needs.

Repair lightly

Copy-and-paste prompt

Using the forward trace and backward trace we just completed, recommend the smallest useful repairs to strengthen this course.

Prioritize changes in this order:

  1. Remove work that does not earn its place.
  2. Merge redundant activities or assignments.
  3. Move a useful task when it appears too early or too late.
  4. Clarify the connection when the learning purpose is sound but invisible to students.
  5. Add a bridge only when an important culminating skill genuinely lacks preparation.

For each recommendation, include:

  • Keep / Remove / Merge / Move / Clarify / Add bridge
  • Course element affected
  • Why the change helps
  • Workload effect — reduces / neutral / adds student or faculty workload
  • Priority — High / Medium / Low

Prefer fewer, higher-impact changes over a long list of improvements.

Do not rebuild successful parts of the course. Do not add an activity simply because a gap could theoretically be filled. Preserve faculty choices that are already working.

End with a short list titled Leave Alone identifying 2–4 parts of the sequence that are already doing their job well and should not be redesigned.

Quick quality check

Does the course feel like a sequence rather than a pile?

✓ Good signs

  • Most meaningful tasks have a clear purpose in the larger learning sequence.
  • Important culminating skills appear before the final high-stakes use.
  • Students get practice before the difficulty jumps.
  • Feedback happens early enough to influence later performance.
  • Practice becomes more independent or complex over time.
  • The course contains breathing room rather than an assignment for every possible need.
  • Removing one task would create a noticeable learning gap—which means that task has a job.

! Watch for

  • An assignment exists because it has always been there, but no one can explain what it builds toward.
  • A major skill appears for the first time inside the assignment that grades it heavily.
  • Several tasks practice essentially the same thing without increasing complexity.
  • Feedback arrives after students no longer have a meaningful chance to use it.
  • A week is busy but has no clear role in the learning arc.
  • AI responds to every gap by adding more work.
  • The course gets stronger only by becoming heavier.

The principle to carry forward

The strongest course may be the one with less in it.

Alignment is not about packing every week. It is about making the important learning visible, connected, practiced, and worth the time. Step 8 zooms back out and audits the finished course against the COR-specific alignment rubric you created at the beginning.
